About

I've spent my career helping organizations turn complicated ideas into things that actually work — enterprise systems, data governance, operating model change, and large technology programs, mostly in places where a mistake has real consequences and nobody gets to move fast and break things.

These days I come at AI from two directions: I build applications myself, and I write about what these systems are doing to work, institutions, and public life. The building keeps the writing honest — it's harder to be glib about what AI can do once you've spent a weekend debugging what it can't.

Projects and Experiments

I build to understand — and to learn what breaks. These projects reflect hands-on experimentation with models, workflows, reliability, privacy, cost, and user experience.

SynthWave Multimodal AI · Full stack · Deployed

Designed and deployed an end-to-end AI application that coordinates text generation, image generation and editing, music providers, media processing, storage, authentication, usage tracking, and progressive previews. The work required continuous comparison of models and providers on quality, latency, reliability, and cost, plus fallbacks and recovery paths when individual services failed.

PhotoCluster Browser ML · Privacy first

Built neural face recognition that runs entirely in the browser: users can group photos by person, name them, and download sorted folders. No server, no uploads, and no tracking — a practical demonstration that useful AI does not have to require surrendering personal data.

Knowledge Compiler AI research workflow · Prototype

Developed an AI-assisted knowledge-synthesis workflow for ingesting long-form sources, extracting and organizing ideas, handling context and rate limits, and turning fragmented material into a navigable knowledge system. The project explored how AI can shorten the loop between information gathering, synthesis, testing, and learning.

Where I Hope to Contribute

I'm not a frontier ML researcher, and I won't pretend otherwise. What I've done for twenty years is take work that is genuinely hard and genuinely valuable and help it survive contact with the real world — with regulators, with finance teams, with the fifty people who have to change how they work on Monday.

Ambitious technical teams usually don't fail at the science. They get slowed down by everything around it: the data nobody trusts, the approval nobody owns, the partner who needs six months of diligence before they'll share a dataset. I find that work genuinely interesting rather than tedious, which I've learned is rarer than it should be.

I've also been building things myself — a music generation app, a browser-based face recognition tool — partly to understand what these systems can actually do, and partly because I enjoy it. It changed how I think about the field. Reading about inference costs is one thing; watching your own API bill climb and having to design your way out of it is another.

At this point in my career I care much more about what I'm working on than what I'm called or what I'm paid. I'd like to spend the next stretch of it on something that matters, with people who want to bring a change to society and help humanity.
